Delhivery Local — Driver App Research Plan

User Research for Full Experience Revamp | August 2026

Research Goal

"Understand how drivers interact with the Delhivery Local app across their daily workflow — their mental models, pain points, workarounds, and unmet needs — to inform a full experience revamp that improves fulfillment rate, reduces cancellations, and increases driver retention."

Research Questions

  1. How do drivers structure their working day, and where does the Delhivery Local app fit alongside other apps (Porter, Rapido, Uber)?
  2. What is the driver's mental model of the app's core flow (broadcast → accept → pickup → deliver → mark complete)?
  3. Where do drivers experience friction, confusion, or anxiety during the order lifecycle?
  4. How do drivers discover and use secondary features (Trip Radar, Slots, Incentives, Performance, Earnings)?
  5. What workarounds have drivers built to compensate for app limitations?
  6. Why do drivers cancel orders, and what conditions lead to repeated cancellations?
  7. What drives a driver to prefer a competitor app over Delhivery Local?

Project Context

Product

Delhivery Local — Intracity delivery driver app

Live for: 2 years

Cities: 6

Drivers: ~7,000+

Business Metrics to Improve

  • Fulfillment rate → >70%
  • Less than 15 min pickup → >70%
  • Deliver on time

  • Improve gross margin

  • Gig workers (multi-app: Porter, Rapido, Uber)
  • Vehicle types: 2W, 3W, Tata Ace, 8ft pickup
  • Age: 25–50 years
  • Tech literacy: Low to medium

Findings from Data

Key insights from analytics data pull (Phase 0 output). These signals shape the hypotheses we'll validate during field research. Data period: Jun 28 – Aug 3, 2026.

Category A Key Business Metrics

1. Fulfillment Rate is a Major Problem

  • Overall fulfillment: ~54.5% — target is >70%. That's a massive gap.
  • Dropped from ~58% in May → ~52-53% in June → slowly recovered to ~55% in late July
  • Nearly half of all orders placed are NOT getting delivered

Research question: Why are orders not getting fulfilled? Is it drivers not accepting broadcasts? Accepting then cancelling? Not enough drivers online?

2. Active Driver Count is Growing, But Fulfillment Isn't

  • Drivers grew from ~8K (late April) to ~29K (late July) — massive growth
  • But fulfillment didn't improve proportionally — more drivers didn't solve the problem
  • Suggests the issue isn't just supply — it's about driver behavior, app experience, or order-driver matching

Research question: If there are enough drivers, why aren't they accepting/completing orders? Is the broadcast UX failing them?

3. City-wise Performance Varies Wildly

4. Pickup Time is Stable but Could Be Better

  • P80 Order to Pickup Arrival: ~21 minutes (consistent across weeks)
  • P80 Pickup Distance: ~3.1–3.6 km
  • Target is <15 min pickup for >70% of orders — currently P80 is 21 min
  • At least 20% of pickups take longer than 21 minutes

Research question: What happens between order acceptance and arriving at pickup? Are drivers finishing a previous delivery? Are they far from pickup? Is navigation an issue?

5. Star vs Non-Star Flexi Drivers — Interesting Split

  • Non-star Flexi riders make up ~70-74% of fulfilled orders
  • Star Flexi riders are ~15-20%
  • Slot riders are ~8-11% (and declining — from ~9.5% to ~7.5%)
  • The Slots feature may not be working well or drivers don't find it valuable

Research question: Do drivers understand the Slots feature? Why are fewer using it? Is guaranteed earning not attractive enough vs. flexible working?

6. WAU Drivers vs Delivered Orders — Efficiency Concern

  • Weekly Active Drivers grew from ~8K to ~29K
  • But "Star" drivers are a tiny fraction (~2-5K out of 25-29K)
  • Most drivers are "Non-star" — meaning they're less reliable/active

Research question: What differentiates a Star driver's behavior from a Non-star? Can the app help non-star drivers perform better?

Updated #1 Research Priority

"Why are drivers in high-demand cities (Bangalore, Delhi, Mumbai) ignoring 10-14 broadcasts before one accepts, when the data shows that faster acceptance directly leads to higher fulfillment?"

The answer likely lives in:

  1. Notification/visibility — drivers don't see the broadcast (app in background, on another app)
  2. Information problem — broadcast doesn't show enough info to decide fast
  3. Incentive problem — fare isn't worth the pickup distance/effort
  4. Trust problem — past bad experiences (long waits, cancellations) make them picky
  5. Multi-app competition — actively on Porter/Rapido, Delhivery is secondary

28. The REAL Cancellation Story — Behavioral Patterns

Pattern A: "Driver gaming the system" (~19%)

"Driver asked me to cancel" = driver doesn't want the order but doesn't want the penalty. Asks customer to cancel instead.

Research question: Why do drivers ask customers to cancel? What's wrong with the order? Is the cancellation penalty too harsh?

Pattern B: "Distance surprise" (~23%)

"Expected shorter pickup time" + "Driver not getting closer" = driver accepts, then realizes actual route (traffic, one-ways) is much longer than straight-line distance shown in broadcast.

Research question: What info does the driver see in the broadcast? Straight-line vs actual route? Estimated time or just km? Does navigation surprise them after accepting?

Pattern C: "Vehicle mismatch" (~20%)

"Requested wrong vehicle" + "Vehicle not suitable" + "Oversized Package" = order shouldn't have come to this driver, or package bigger than described.

Research question: Is the consumer misrepresenting package size? Or is the system broadcasting to wrong vehicle types? Do drivers feel forced to accept orders that won't fit?

Top Interview Questions (Ranked by Data Impact)

  1. "Why do you ask the customer to cancel instead of cancelling yourself?" (18.86% of cancellations)
  2. "Has the pickup ever been farther than you expected after accepting?" (12.61%)

Key Takeaways for Partner Performance

  • 5% acceptance rate is abysmal — 95% of broadcasts are ignored/rejected. The broadcast experience needs fundamental rethinking.
  • Star drivers earn 2-3x more because they accept 2-3x more — the behavior gap is about acceptance willingness, not hours online.
  • Non-Star drivers logging 5+ hrs but delivering 0.3 orders/day are clearly using the app passively or as backup to other platforms.
  • Jaipur's acceptance rate (20-30%) vs Bangalore/Delhi (3-7%) — city-level dynamics play a huge role.
  • 4-5K drivers log in for <1 hour daily — understand why they leave so quickly.

Phase 1: Field Research with Drivers

Week 2: Aug 11–15 Week 3: Aug 18–22

The core of the research. Three complementary methods to build a complete picture.

Primary Method

Method 1: Contextual Inquiry / Ride-Alongs

What: Shadow drivers during their actual working hours with an ops team member as translator.

Why: See real behavior — how they hold the phone while riding, when they check the app, what confuses them, what workarounds they've developed.

Duration: 2–3 hours per driver

Sample: 8–10 drivers

What to observe:

  • Physical setup (phone mount, how they manage cash, vehicle condition)
  • App usage patterns during idle/waiting time
  • Decision-making process when broadcast comes in (accept/reject criteria)
  • Navigation behavior (do they use in-app nav, Google Maps, or memory?)
  • Pickup and drop-off process (COD cash handling, OTP, proof of delivery)
  • How they switch between multiple apps
  • Emotional states throughout the journey (stress points, satisfaction moments)
  • Interactions with customers and merchants

Questions to ask during ride-along:

  • "Walk me through your morning when you start working"
  • "How do you decide which orders to accept?"
  • "When was the last time you were frustrated with this app?"
  • "Show me how you check your earnings"
  • "What do you do when there are no orders?"
  • "Why do you sometimes cancel an order after accepting?"
  • "What do you like better about [Porter/Rapido]?"
Secondary Method

Method 2: In-Office Usability Testing

What: Bring drivers to office and give them task-based scenarios on the current app.

Why: Structured way to identify specific usability issues per screen/flow.

Duration: 45–60 minutes per session

Sample: 5–6 drivers (different from ride-along participants)

Task Scenarios:

  1. "Show me how you would check your earnings from last week"
  2. "You want to work in a specific time slot tomorrow — how would you do that?"
  3. "Show me where you can see available trips nearby"
  4. "How would you check if you're close to earning an incentive today?"
  5. "Walk me through what happens from when you get an order to marking it delivered"
  6. "Show me how you'd check your performance rating"

Protocol: Think-aloud (translated by ops person). Record screen + audio with consent.

Supplementary

Method 3: Quick Intercept Surveys

What: Short 5-minute structured questionnaire at driver hubs/hotspots.

Why: Get broader quantitative signals on satisfaction, feature awareness, and competitor usage.

Sample: 20–30 drivers (can be administered by ops team via Google Form)

Survey Topics:

  • Overall app satisfaction (1–5 scale)
  • Feature awareness checklist (do they know Trip Radar, Slots, Incentives exist?)
  • Top 3 frustrations (open-ended)
  • Other apps they use and why
  • What one thing they'd change about the app
  • How they learned to use the app

Phase 2: Synthesis & Analysis

Week 3–4: Aug 22–29

Transform raw observations into actionable design insights.

1. Affinity Mapping

Cluster all observations, quotes, and notes into themes.

Example themes might include:

  • Navigation confusion
  • Cash management stress
  • Feature discovery issues
  • Broadcast decision-making anxiety
  • Earnings transparency gaps
  • Multi-app juggling patterns

2. Journey Mapping

Map the end-to-end driver journey with these layers:

  • Stages: Start day → Wait for orders → Accept → Pickup → Transit → Deliver → Review earnings → End day
  • Actions: What they do at each stage
  • Touchpoints: App screens, calls, physical interactions
  • Pain points: Frustrations and friction
  • Emotions: Stress, confusion, satisfaction, anxiety
  • Opportunities: Where design can help

3. Behavioral Personas

Create 2–3 driver personas based on behavior patterns (not just demographics).

Potential persona axes:

  • Maximizer (optimizes every order for maximum earnings) vs. Steady earner (consistent pace, fewer risks)
  • Tech-comfortable vs. Tech-minimal
  • Delhivery-primary vs. Multi-app juggler

4. Opportunity Scoring

Prioritize problems using a simple framework:

CriteriaScale
Frequency (how often does this happen?)1–5
Severity (how painful when it happens?)1–5
Business Impact (does fixing this move our metrics?)1–5

Score = Frequency × Severity × Business Impact

5. Insight Statements

Format each finding as:

"We observed [behavior], because [underlying reason], which means [implication for design]."

Example: "We observed drivers checking Porter between Delhivery broadcasts, because they fear idle time means lost income, which means our idle state needs to proactively surface earning opportunities."

Tips & Considerations

Managing the Language Barrier

  • Brief your translator beforehand — tell them what you're looking for, not just to translate literally. Share key questions in advance.
  • Use visual methods — ask drivers to "show me" rather than "tell me." Screen recordings capture behavior regardless of language.
  • Create a simple observation template — note body language, hesitations, tap patterns, and workarounds that don't need verbal explanation.
  • Card sorting exercises — visual and less language-dependent. Great for understanding mental models of information architecture.
  • Record everything — you can get detailed translations later from recordings.

Ride-Along Best Practices

  • Don't interrupt the flow — observe first, ask questions during natural breaks (waiting for next order, after a delivery)
  • Note the environment — noise, weather, traffic. These affect app usability on mid-range phones.
  • Pay attention to what they do between orders — this reveals unmet needs
  • Ask "why" gently — "I noticed you did X, can you tell me why?" rather than "why did you do that?"
  • Document their phone setup — screen brightness, notification settings, other apps open

Presenting to Stakeholders

  • Lead with video clips — 30-second driver clips are more persuasive than any slide
  • Connect every insight to a business metric — stakeholders care about fulfillment rate and cancellations
  • Use the "Now, Next, Later" framework to make findings actionable immediately
  • Create a shareable 1-page summary for leadership who won't read the full doc
  • Include competitor comparisons — "Porter does X differently, and drivers prefer it because Y"

Things to Watch For (Specific to Your Context)

  • COD friction: 80% of orders are COD — observe cash handling pain points
  • Broadcast anxiety: Orders expire fast — watch for stress during accept/reject decisions
  • Multi-app switching: Drivers use Porter/Rapido simultaneously — observe the switching pattern
  • Homepage confusion: 5 CTAs + random feature entry points — watch for navigation failures
  • Slot booking behavior: Do they plan ahead or book last-minute?
  • Incentive understanding: Do they actually know what targets to hit?
